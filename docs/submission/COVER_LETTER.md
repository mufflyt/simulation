Dear Editor,

Please consider the enclosed manuscript, "Historical Validation of National
Urogynecology Workforce Projections: When the Validation Target Does Not Match
the Workforce," for publication as Original Research.

Whether the United States will have enough urogynecologists to care for an aging
female population is an active question for the subspecialty, and projections
are increasingly cited in discussions of fellowship training and access to
pelvic-floor care. Those projections are rarely tested against what subsequently
happened. In a systematic review of 40 health workforce projection studies, only
8 compared their predictions with historical data and only 4 conducted external
validation.

We built a national microsimulation of the urogynecology and reconstructive
pelvic surgery workforce and asked whether it reproduces the observed number of
board-certified urogynecologists when forecasting from historical data alone. It
does not, and the reason is the point of the paper. Fifty-nine percent of the
99-provider discrepancy arose not from assumptions about how urogynecologists
retire or how many enter the field, but because the model counted clinically
active physicians while the national series used to check it counts cumulative
certifications, from which no one is ever removed. The two quantities are both
defensible and are not the same.

We think this will interest your readers for three reasons. First, it is
directly relevant to how the subspecialty should read the workforce projections
now circulating: a model can represent physician behavior faithfully and still
produce a misleading number for fellowship planning. Second, the conventional
way of judging such a model, whether its interval contains the observed value,
ranked highest the forecast that a proper scoring rule ranked last: one whose
interval was 1,466 providers wide with a lower bound below zero. Third, apparent accuracy proved to depend heavily on
validation design: the identical model appeared 2.7 times closer to the observed
series when the validation admitted information unavailable at the forecast
origin.

We are explicit about what the study cannot claim. There is no gold-standard
external measure of the clinically active urogynecology workforce; the only
national series is cumulative certification, so all results are agreement
against an imperfect benchmark. We report this as a demonstration of what
happens when such a benchmark is treated as truth, not as a validated forecast.
The repeated-target evidence rests on four rolling origins and is diagnostic
rather than a precise estimate of long-run interval calibration.

The manuscript is 2,141 words of main text with a 294-word structured abstract,
two tables, no figures, and one supplemental digital content file. It has not
been published elsewhere and is not under consideration by another journal. All
analyses are reproducible from committed code and artifacts. Every model
parameter in the historical forecast was audited for availability at the 2020
origin; the one parameter that postdated it was removed from the primary
analysis and is reported only as a sensitivity analysis.

Thank you for your consideration.
